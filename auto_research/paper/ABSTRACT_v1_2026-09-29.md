## Abstract (v1)

A world model has two functional parts: an encoder that represents what is observed, and a predictor that carries the world forward when observation stops. Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPA) are credited with intuitive physics and state-of-the-art action anticipation, and both readings attribute the world model to the encoder–predictor pair. We ask which part actually holds the knowledge. With a label-free, scene-level readout calibrated on synthetic ground truth and applied to real video, we find that the roles are inverted. The frozen encoder already carries what prediction needs—identity, velocity, and where every future patch was last seen—while the pretrained predictor does not evolve a state: it retrieves content from the last observation and places it only a bounded distance away (2–5 cells), leaves occluded content behind, and cannot consume its own outputs as observations. The benchmarks that motivate the world-model reading do not detect this: copying the last latent matches their scores within a few points, and removing the predictor barely changes anticipation. Nor does the objective alone repair it—predictors retrained on real video with future-only or autoregressive objectives extend transport only in-domain, keep no occluded content, and collapse from the first self-fed step. We therefore (i) propose real-video protocols that measure the vacant capability directly (reach, permanence, closure, and copy-normalized downstream deltas), and ★ (ii) train a new predictor on a frozen encoder from real video, designed from these failures—separating what is carried from where it goes, and separating the carried state from the rendered output. ★ It extends reach and permanence on real video without losing existing benchmark accuracy and improves long-horizon anticipation on EK100 and occlusion cases on IntPhys 2. World knowledge belongs in the predictor; we show it is not there yet, how to measure it, and ★ how to put it there.
