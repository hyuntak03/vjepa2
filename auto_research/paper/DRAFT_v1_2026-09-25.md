## Abstract

Latent video world models divide the work between an encoder that represents what is observed and a predictor that advances that representation when observations stop.
The pretrained V-JEPA 2 predictor is used as such a world model in intuitive-physics evaluations and downstream uses, although it was pretrained to fill space-time tube masks rather than to predict the future.
We ask whether it evolves the latent state beyond what it last observed, and where the process breaks.
With controlled synthetic motion, parameter-free readouts, positional nulls, copy and oracle baselines, and trajectory-level statistics — plus real videos — we find:
(i) the frozen encoder exposes the state the predictor needs: velocity and motion direction are linearly readable from the tokens the predictor consumes, and motion history is summarized into the most recent (boundary) tokens;
(ii) the pretrained predictor extrapolates only a few steps from those boundary tokens — the next step is set by the last token alone, predicted objects are pulled back toward past observed positions, and they fade with time since the last observation; fed its own outputs, it stops advancing;
(iii) predictors trained for future prediction on the *same frozen encoder* — including one trained only on natural video — carry the last-seen object to its true location far beyond the released predictor, matching an extrapolator built on encoder-read velocity, so the encoder is not the bottleneck;
(iv) violation-of-expectation surprise does not track this ability: the predictor that evolves the state best scores lowest on IntPhys, and copying the last latent nearly matches the released predictor there.
We turn these diagnostics into a signature-based test suite and design requirements for predictors that must evolve state when they cannot see.

## 1. Introduction

**World model = read + evolve.** A latent world model maintains a belief state sufficient for predicting the latent future and advances it with a transition that stays predictive when no new observation arrives [POMDP/PSR; PlaNet; LeCun 2022].
Generative video world models were recently shown to freeze or reset unobserved states [StEvo-Bench; WRBench]. Latent predictive models make the division of labour explicit — encoder reads, predictor evolves — which lets us localize the failure.

**Why the pretrained predictor.** It is used as a world model:
- VoE surprise on IntPhys and IntPhys 2 [Garrido et al. 2025; Bordes et al. 2025];
- forecasting features for EK100 anticipation [V-JEPA 2];
- surprise-based rewards and guidance [WMReward; Off-Manifold Refinement];
- memory adapters built on it [HERA].

Its pretraining masks span the whole clip (`temporal_scale [1,1]`), so future-only queries are outside its training distribution. We treat this as an evaluation-validity question — evaluations attribute world knowledge to a module used out of regime — not as the explanation of the failures (§8).
Planning in V-JEPA 2-AC trains a new predictor; we make no planning claim.

**The open question.** IntPhys 1 is near ceiling while IntPhys 2 is near chance, and IntPhys 2 reviewers asked: "Is it due to memory? Or representation and perception?". We answer at the component level.

**Contributions.**
1. A diagnostic suite for latent state evolution. It combines parameter-free template readouts with positional nulls, copy / oracle / constant-velocity baselines, closure tests (feeding back observations vs. own predictions through the same interface), history and boundary patching with timing twins, and trajectory-cluster statistics.
2. A behavioural and causal account of the pretrained predictor: few-step extrapolation from the last token, backward pull by history tokens, and fading with time since the last observation.
3. A constructive localization: with the encoder fixed, predictors trained for future prediction recover far-horizon placement up to an encoder-state extrapolator. The encoder suffices for this regime; the pretrained predictor is the bottleneck.
4. An evaluation audit: surprise accuracy is non-monotone in evolution ability across predictors; copy baselines and bidirectional-target leakage explain much of the IntPhys score.

**Non-obvious findings (page 1).**
- (a) One-step transitions exist: fresh observations advance the object every step.
- (b) The step is read from the last token: a history made entirely of the model's own predictions is harmless if the last token is a real observation, and a single self-predicted last token slows the step.
- (c) The same frozen encoder supports far longer evolution when the predictor is trained for it, even out of domain.

## 3. Setup and readouts

- **Model.** Released V-JEPA 2 ViT-H (fpc64, 256 px). The context encoder is the online encoder; the target is the EMA encoder with affine-free LayerNorm. The predictor has 12 blocks and uses mask token 0. Scoring numerics are fp32 with fp16 autocast.
- **Predictors compared (same frozen encoder, no new training):**
  - released;
  - `predictor_v1_postft` (released predictor fine-tuned future-only on synthetic motion close to our test domain);
  - Ariel block-causal (trained from scratch, future-only, on SSv2 + K400; block-causal attention);
  - `v11_postft` (future-only fine-tune on IntPhysGen v11).
- **Data.**
  - RollOut_v3: 64 frames at 30 fps, 8 motion laws. Free-motion clips are 84 trajectories × 28 appearances, so CIs are trajectory-cluster bootstraps.
  - IntPhysGen v11: pixel-matched quadruplets with occlusion-timing twins.
  - IntPhys 1 dev (Garrido protocol).
  - SSv2 left/right motion: 677 clips.
- **Readouts (validated).**
  - Template argmax against the true-cell token of the standard target, reported as hit − other-trajectory null. Truth-cell templates can be pulled by background, so we always pair them with an appearance template: the last-seen object token, which uses no future truth.
  - Along-track progress.
  - Objectness at one common threshold.
  - Token-level slot-to-slot change regression β = ρ × gain on SSv2.
- **Baselines and oracles.**
  - Copy of the last latent (standard and causal target).
  - Constant velocity from encoder-read velocity.
  - Freeze-after-K oracle.
  - Blurred and error-matched progression oracles on SSv2.

## 4. The encoder exposes the state

- **4.1 Readable at the interface.**
  - Velocity from the tokens the predictor consumes (z, last context tubelet): pooled R² 0.985 / 0.976, within law × trajectory-group speed R² 0.955, trajectory-disjoint CV [V].
  - SSv2 motion direction from z: 0.89–0.97, against a pixel baseline of 0.41–0.70 [V].
  - Acceleration is not reliably readable at short context [V, H1].
- **4.2 History is summarized into boundary tokens.** Boundary tokens encoded without seeing earlier frames (position-preserving mask) lose far-slot progress: t8 along-track −0.47 [−0.57, −0.36] cells. Adding the predictor-level history tokens back does not restore it (−0.52) [1st, M6].
- **4.3 Sufficiency.** Extrapolating at constant velocity from the last observed position, with velocity read from z, reaches 0.74 at t10, where the released predictor reaches 0.08 (null 0.20) [V]. Caveat: a label-mean velocity already gives 0.61, so the z-specific gain is +0.14 [V].
- **4.4 The same encoder supports long evolution** (constructive; §7).

## 5. The pretrained predictor extrapolates a few steps and fades

- **5.1 Horizon.**
  - Far-slot object signal decays and position lags constant-velocity extrapolation (integration gain ≈ 0.4–0.7) [V].
  - Stride 1/2/4 rejects a fixed tubelet budget [V]. In position readouts the curve is described by a few-step extrapolation plus readout tolerance; a freeze-after-3-steps oracle fits it (R² 0.99) [V].
  - Presence (objectness) fades with time since the last observation [V].
- **5.2 Real video.**
  - SSv2 predicted slots follow the true time for ~3 slots: null-corrected diagonal .62 / .32 / .33 / .21, then .10–.22 [V].
  - Token-level slot-to-slot changes are ~15 % of the true magnitude, with direction correlation 0.16 → 0.05 [1st].
  - An error-matched progressing oracle stays perfectly aligned, so the decline reflects missing progression rather than error magnitude [1st].
- **5.3 Self-feedback stalls; the step is read from the last token** [1st, P3b; slopes vs. real-observation feedback 0.361, truth 0.440]:
  - causal target observations through the same adapter: +0.000;
  - true history with only the last token self-predicted: −0.102;
  - self-predicted history with a true last token: +0.023;
  - own chain: −0.373;
  - tokens encoded with the whole window (true, but time-mixed): −0.13 to −0.15.
- **5.4 No inference-time use extends the horizon:** single query, layer lens, attention knockout [V, H4/H23/H8].

## 6. Where the object comes from: causal localization

- **6.1 Presence is set at the boundary** (v11 timing twins, moving objects) [V]. Swapping only the last 1–2 context tubelets transfers the late/early presence gap (recovery 0.94 [0.91, 0.97]). Position recovers much less (0.27). Static objects are maintained from history (destruction 0.36).
- **6.2 Future object content is retrieved from context object tokens** [V, M4]:
  - object-selective attention: 3–13× uniform, against 0.4–1.1× for same-distance non-object keys;
  - direct future → context retrieval is necessary at displacement ≤ 4 cells (restricting it collapses hits);
  - reach declines with both distance and slot. There is no fixed radius.
- **6.3 History tokens pull predictions backward** [1st, M6]. Shifting the same clip's history tokens 8 / 4 frames into the past or 4 frames forward moves far-slot predictions monotonically: t6 −0.33 / −0.22 / +0.34 cells, CIs exclude 0 at t6–t10.
- **6.4 The velocity code is used for one step only** [1st, M5]. Steering the boundary object tokens along the ridge velocity direction moves the first predicted tubelet (gain ≈ 0.4). Later slots do not accumulate the shift (gain ≤ 0.13 after t1); a random direction of equal norm does nothing.
- **Synthesis.** The encoder integrates motion into the boundary tokens. The pretrained predictor reads one step of motion from the last token, fills the future by retrieving context object content (anchored to observed, partly past positions), and has no carried state that its own outputs can continue.

## 7. The encoder is enough: constructive localization with trained predictors [1st]

v3, stride 1, C16/P32, same frozen encoder:

| | released | predictor_v1_postft | Ariel (natural video only) | v11_postft |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| t10 hit − null (true-cell template) | −0.12 | +0.23 | +0.24 | +0.07 |
| t10 appearance-template hit (encoder ceiling 0.80) | 0.04 | 0.67 | 0.73 | 0.35 |
| along-track progress t10 (truth = 1) | 0.42 | 0.92 | 0.93 | 0.87 (overshoots early) |
| t10 hit vs. encoder-velocity extrapolator 0.74 | 0.08 | 0.70 | 0.74 | 0.30 |
| self-feedback slope (truth 0.44) | −0.01 | 0.14 | 0.23 | 0.15 |
| IntPhys 1 (Garrido A.8 best) | 88.89 | 80.56 | 69.44 | 80.83 |

- **Reading.**
  - With the encoder fixed, predictors trained for future prediction place the last-seen object near its true location far beyond the released predictor, up to the encoder-state extrapolator. This holds even when training data (natural video) differs from the test domain (synthetic).
  - Training alone is not sufficient: `v11_postft` overshoots at stride 1.
  - All four predictors remain insensitive to swapping earlier history with a different trajectory (≈0). The released predictor's step is set by its last token.
- **Caveats.** Data, architecture (block-causal), initialization and training stride differ jointly. Presence readouts (objectness; the attention rulers of a parallel analysis) give weaker or mixed gains for Ariel. No predictor is shown to add acceleration.

## 8. Consequences for evaluation

- **Surprise accuracy is non-monotone in evolution ability:** Ariel scores best in §7 and lowest on IntPhys 1 (69.44 vs 88.89) [1st].
- **Copy baselines** [V]:
  - copying the last latent reaches 85.00 on IntPhys 1 (best cell), against 88.89 for the released predictor (n.s.; official cell skip2_w16 +12.2);
  - on v11 the released margin over copy (+2.5) comes entirely from color violations (parallel analysis).
- **Bidirectional target leakage:** pre-event tubelets discriminate at 90.4 % with the standard target, against 50.0 % with a causal target [V].
- **L1 rewards blur:** on SSv2, 61–79 % of the predictor's advantage over copying is reproduced by blurred copies [V].
- **Recommended protocol:**
  - horizon curves in cells and seconds, reported with nulls;
  - copy, constant-velocity and freeze-K oracles;
  - a closure test;
  - causal targets;
  - appearance-template readouts.

## 9. Toward predictors that evolve state

Each requirement is tied to the signature it must remove:

| requirement | signature to remove | evidence |
|---|---|---|
| carried state that is not a function of the last token alone | step set by the last token; self-feedback stall | P3b; §7 slopes ≤ 0.23 even after training |
| future-only multi-step training with self-feedback | self-feedback stall; fading | §7 (trained predictors improve) |
| retrieval not anchored to past observed positions | backward pull (lag) | M6 |
| causal targets and time-consistent inputs | window-encoded tokens slow the step; leakage | P3b; H6 |
| evaluation by signatures, not surprise accuracy | VoE non-monotonicity | §8 |

## 10. Limitations

- **Scope.**
  - One encoder (ViT-H); replication on other scales and V-JEPA 2.1 is on hold.
  - Synthetic-heavy, with an SSv2 anchor that is inside the pretraining distribution.
  - Kinematics only; acceleration is not transferred by any predictor.
- **Occlusion at the context boundary is a corner case.** The central claims hold without occlusion (v3).
- **The trained-predictor comparison confounds several factors.**
- **Readout dependence.** Truth-cell templates can be pulled by background; presence and position readouts can disagree.
- **Hedging vs. anchoring.** Deterministic L1 predictors hedge. We argue behaviourally (lag toward past observed positions, M6) and leave the objective to a discussion paragraph.
